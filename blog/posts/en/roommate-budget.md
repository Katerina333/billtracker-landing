A roommate budget works when everyone agrees on three things before the first bill arrives: which costs are shared, how each one is split, and who pays and records what. Put the shared costs into a few categories with a monthly amount, have everyone log what they buy for the house, and settle up once a month. Most money fights between roommates come from skipping one of those three steps, not from the amounts themselves.

## What a roommate budget should cover

Start by drawing the line between shared and personal money. Anything nobody has agreed to share stays personal — that one rule prevents a lot of arguments.

**Usually shared:**

- Rent (if there's one lease or one landlord)
- Electricity, gas and water, if they aren't included in the rent
- Internet and any TV licence or streaming plan the whole house uses
- Shared groceries and basics: milk, bread, oil, coffee, toilet paper
- Cleaning and household supplies: washing-up liquid, bin bags, sponges

**Usually personal:**

- Your own food, snacks and drinks
- Your phone plan, transport and subscriptions only you use
- Anything for your own room

Grey areas — a new vacuum cleaner, a houseplant, a shared dinner for someone's birthday — get a quick "shared or not?" before anyone buys. Write the list down in a note or the house group chat so new roommates can read it too.

## How to split bills with roommates: three fair ways

There is no single fair split; there is the one you all agreed to. Three common ones:

1. **Equal shares.** Everyone pays the same. Simple, and fine when the rooms and the use of the flat are similar.
2. **By room.** Rent follows room size or features — the bigger room or the one with a balcony pays more. Bills and groceries stay equal.
3. **By use.** Each person pays for what they use, for example a separate shelf for groceries. Fair, but it takes more tracking.

Most shared homes mix them: rent by room, everything else equally.

## A worked example: three roommates

Sam, Alex and Jo rent a three-bedroom flat. They agreed to split the rent by room size and everything else equally. Their monthly roommate budget:

| Shared cost | Monthly amount | How it's split |
|---|---|---|
| Rent | 2,100 | by room |
| Electricity & gas | 150 | equally |
| Internet | 45 | equally |
| Shared groceries & basics | 300 | equally |
| Cleaning & household | 45 | equally |
| **Total** | **2,640** | |

The rooms are priced at 800, 700 and 600, which adds up to the 2,100 rent. The other shared costs come to 540, so each person's equal part is 180:

| Roommate | Rent | Shared costs | Monthly share |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sam (largest room) | 800 | 180 | 980 |
| Alex | 700 | 180 | 880 |
| Jo (smallest room) | 600 | 180 | 780 |
| **Total** | **2,100** | **540** | **2,640** |

Everyone sees in advance what living there costs them each month, and nobody has to remember who agreed to what.

## How to split groceries with roommates

Groceries are where shared money gets messy, because everyone buys a little, all the time. Two setups work well:

- **A kitty.** Everyone puts the same amount into a shared pot — a jar, a shared account or one person's card — and shared groceries are paid only from it. When it's empty, you top it up together.
- **Buy and log.** Whoever is at the shop buys for the house and logs the amount in a shared "Groceries" category. At the end of the month you compare who paid what.

Either way, keep the shared list short: the basics everyone uses. Personal food stays on personal shelves. Our guide to setting a [grocery budget](/blog/grocery-budget-for-a-family/) shows how to pick a realistic monthly amount.

## Settle up once a month, by hand

If you use "buy and log", settling up is a five-minute job. Take what each person spent on shared groceries this month, work out the fair share, and look at the difference:

| Roommate | Paid | Fair share | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sam | 132 | 92 | +40 |
| Alex | 96 | 92 | +4 |
| Jo | 48 | 92 | -44 |
| **Total** | **276** | **276** | **0** |

Jo pays 40 to Sam and 4 to Alex, and the month is closed. If you split by income or by room instead of equally, the same table works — only the fair-share column changes. Our guide on [splitting costs proportionally](/blog/budgeting-for-couples-with-different-incomes/) shows that maths step by step.

Do it on the same day every month — rent day is easy to remember — so small amounts never pile up into an awkward conversation.

## Roommate agreement rules that keep the money calm

- **Ask before big shared purchases.** Anything above an amount you agree on needs a yes from everyone.
- **Bills in one name get logged.** If the internet contract is in Sam's name, Sam logs it every month so the others see it was paid.
- **Guests and partners.** Agree what happens when someone stays over often — does it change the grocery split?
- **Running out.** The person who uses the last toilet roll puts it on the list or buys the next pack.
- **Moving out.** Settle up in full before someone leaves, including bills that arrive after they've gone.

## FAQ

### What is a good roommate budget template?

A list of shared costs with a monthly amount and how each one is split — like the first table above — plus a simple log of who paid what. You can keep it in a spreadsheet or use a shared budget app where everyone logs on their own phone.

### Should rent be split equally between roommates?

It depends on what you agree. Equal rent is simple when the rooms are similar. If one room is clearly bigger or has its own bathroom, many shared homes give it a higher share and split everything else equally.

### How do roommates split utilities?

Most split electricity, gas, water and internet equally, because everyone uses them. Log each bill when it's paid so everyone can see the amount, and settle the shares at the monthly check-in.

### How often should roommates settle up?

Once a month is enough for most homes. Pick a fixed day, such as rent day, and settle everything at once instead of chasing small amounts every week.
