Budgeting for couples with different incomes starts with one decision: how much each of you puts towards the shared costs. The three common answers are 50/50, in proportion to income, or everything into one pot. Pick the one that feels fair to both of you, write the numbers down once a month, and run one shared budget for the shared costs — the split itself only takes a few minutes.

What tends to go wrong isn't the maths. It's that the rule was never said out loud. One person quietly pays more, the other quietly feels judged, and every bigger purchase reopens the question. The goal of this guide is to settle it once.

## Why a 50/50 split often feels unfair

Splitting everything down the middle sounds neutral. With equal incomes, it is. With different incomes, it means the person who earns less spends a much bigger share of their pay on rent and groceries — and has far less left for everything else.

Take Alex and Sam, who bring home 3,000 and 2,000 a month and share 2,500 of costs. At 50/50, each pays 1,250. Alex keeps 1,750; Sam keeps 750. They live in the same home, eat the same food, and one of them has more than twice as much left over. Some couples are fine with that. Many find, after a few months, that it shapes every decision: which restaurant, which holiday, who says no.

## 3 ways to split bills when incomes differ

### 1. Equal split (50/50)

Each person pays half of every shared cost.

- **Works when:** incomes are close, or you've only just moved in together and want a simple start.
- **Watch out for:** a big income gap. The lower earner ends up with little room for anything personal.

### 2. Proportional split

Each person pays the same *percentage* of their income. If one earns 60% of the household total, they pay 60% of the shared costs.

- **Works when:** you want both people to keep the same share of their own pay.
- **Watch out for:** recalculating when an income changes — a new job, parental leave, a bonus month.

### 3. One pot

All income goes into one budget. Shared costs come out first, then each person gets the same amount of personal money, whatever they earned.

- **Works when:** you think of yourselves as one household, especially with kids or when one of you works fewer hours.
- **Watch out for:** it only works if both of you see every number. One pot kept by one person tends to feel like an allowance.

There's no right answer, only the one you both agree on. A useful test: imagine the higher earner losing their job next year. Would the rule still feel fair?

## A worked example: proportional split, step by step

Here's the proportional method with real numbers. Alex and Sam again, with monthly take-home pay of 3,000 and 2,000.

**Step 1 — list the shared costs.** Only what you both use: rent, utilities, groceries, internet, shared insurance, the car if you share it. Personal things — your own phone, clothes, hobbies — stay out.

| Shared cost | Per month |
|---|---|
| Rent | 1,400 |
| Utilities and internet | 230 |
| Groceries | 600 |
| Transport | 170 |
| Household and repairs | 100 |
| **Total** | **2,500** |

**Step 2 — work out the shares.** Combined income is 5,000. The first person earns 3,000 ÷ 5,000 = 60%; the second 2,000 ÷ 5,000 = 40%.

**Step 3 — apply the shares to the shared costs.**

| Person | Take-home pay | Share | Pays into shared costs | Left for personal spending |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alex | 3,000 | 60% | 1,500 | 1,500 |
| Sam | 2,000 | 40% | 1,000 | 1,000 |
| **Total** | **5,000** | **100%** | **2,500** | **2,500** |

Both now spend exactly half of their pay on the household. Compare that with the 50/50 split above, where Sam would have kept only 750.

**Step 4 — decide what "personal" covers.** Clothes, gifts for your own family, hobbies, your own savings — whatever you agree. Many couples also set a rule for big purchases: anything over an agreed amount gets mentioned first, whoever pays.

## Running the shared budget month to month

The split tells you who pays in. A shared budget tells you where it goes. Once the contributions are clear, the rest is the same as for any household:

- **Give each shared cost an envelope** with a monthly amount, like the table above. Our guide to [envelope budgeting for couples](/blog/envelope-budgeting-for-couples/) walks through setting the amounts.
- **Both of you log what you spend from the shared money**, right after paying. A budget only one person updates is always out of date.
- **Check in for ten minutes a week.** Is groceries ahead of the calendar? Is anything coming up?
- **Recalculate the split when an income changes**, not every month. Write down the date and the new percentages so nobody has to remember.

## Common mistakes

- **Never saying the rule out loud.** "We just pay for things" works until the first big bill. Write the split down.
- **Counting personal costs as shared.** If one person's car, gym or student loan sits in the shared list, the percentages stop meaning anything.
- **Keeping score of every coffee.** A proportional split is for the big, regular costs. Small things even out.
- **Forgetting unpaid work.** If one of you works part-time to cover childcare or care for a parent, a pure income split may not feel fair. That's often where couples move to one pot.
- **Not revisiting it.** A rule that fitted when you moved in may not fit after a promotion or a baby.

## FAQ

### What is the fairest way to split bills with different incomes?

There isn't one fairest way, only the one both partners agree is fair. Many couples with a noticeable income gap choose a proportional split, so each pays the same percentage of their pay towards shared costs and keeps the same percentage for themselves.

### How do you split expenses proportionally to income?

Add up both take-home incomes, divide each person's income by the total to get their share, then multiply the monthly shared costs by that share. With 3,000 and 2,000, the shares are 60% and 40%, so 2,500 of shared costs becomes 1,500 and 1,000.

### Do we need a joint account to budget together?

No. You can each pay your share from your own account and still keep one shared budget. What matters is that both of you see the same numbers for the shared costs.

### Should savings be split proportionally too?

That's a decision for the two of you. Some couples treat shared savings like a shared cost and split it by the same percentages; others save separately from their personal money. Either works if it's agreed.
