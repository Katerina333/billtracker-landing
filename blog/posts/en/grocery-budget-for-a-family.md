The right grocery budget for a family is the one that comes from your own receipts, not from someone else's average. Add up what you actually spent on groceries in the last two months, trim it gently, and split the monthly amount into weekly limits. Then log every shop and check the number once a week — that's what makes it last to payday.

Groceries are one of the envelopes families blow most easily, for a simple reason: it's spent in many small amounts, by several people, and nobody sees the total until it's gone. The steps below fix exactly that.

## Why there's no "right" grocery budget for a family of 4

Searches for "grocery budget for a family of 4" usually hope for one number. There isn't a useful one. Where you live, how old the kids are, how much you cook, whether lunch is eaten at school or packed at home, dietary needs — each of these moves the total more than family size does.

An average can still be a rough cross-check, but your own spending is a better starting point for two reasons: it already reflects your shop, your prices and your kids' appetites, and it's hard to fail against a number you've already lived with.

## Step 1: Add up two months of real grocery spending

Go through your last two months of card statements and receipts and pull out every grocery purchase. Sort them into a few groups so you can see where the money went:

- **The big weekly shop**
- **Top-up shops** — bread, milk, "just a few things"
- **Toiletries and cleaning** — if you buy them with the food
- **Snacks on the go** — the bakery, the petrol station, the vending machine

Decide now what counts as groceries and stick to it. Eating out and takeaway belong in their own envelope; otherwise the grocery number will never make sense.

| Grocery spending | September | October |
|---|---|---|
| Big weekly shops | 640 | 610 |
| Top-up shops | 210 | 190 |
| Toiletries and cleaning | 90 | 80 |
| Snacks on the go | 80 | 60 |
| **Total** | **1,020** | **940** |

The average is 980 a month. That is what this family really spends — before any plan.

## Step 2: Set the monthly grocery budget

Start from your average and trim where the money leaks, not where the food is. In the example, the big shops are steady; top-ups and snacks are where the month goes off track. A cut of about 3% — 30 a month — taken from those two groups is realistic. Cutting 30% on paper usually means a blown envelope by the third week.

So the family sets a **monthly grocery budget of 950**. For a family of four that's about 240 per person a month — a handy figure to compare months later, or to adjust when a baby arrives or a teenager starts eating twice as much.

## Step 3: Split it into weekly amounts

A monthly limit is hard to feel on a Tuesday. A weekly one isn't. Count the weeks from your payday and give each one an amount:

| Week | Grocery budget |
|---|---|
| Week 1 (stock-up after payday) | 270 |
| Week 2 | 210 |
| Week 3 | 210 |
| Week 4 | 210 |
| Days 29–31 | 50 |
| **Total** | **950** |

The first week gets more because that's when the cupboards get refilled. If your budget month runs from payday to payday rather than from the 1st, the weeks line up with when the money actually arrives.

If a week ends under budget, leave the difference in the envelope — it's your buffer for the week with a birthday cake or a school bake sale.

## Step 4: Log every shop, by whoever shops

The plan only works if the envelope shows the truth. That means every grocery purchase gets logged, ideally at the till, by whoever paid. In families, the top-up shops are the problem: one parent buys milk on the way home, the other buys bread at lunch, and neither counts as "real" shopping.

- **Log right after paying.** It takes seconds and nobody has to remember on Sunday.
- **Both adults log.** A grocery envelope only one person updates is always behind.
- **Keep receipts for one week** if you're new to it, and compare them with your log at the weekly check.

## Step 5: A 10-minute grocery check every week

Once a week, look at three things:

1. **Pace.** Is the envelope roughly where the weeks say it should be? If it's day 14 of your budget month and 65% is gone, the next two weeks need a plan, not hope.
2. **Top-ups.** How many small shops were there? Five top-ups at 15 often cost more than one forgotten item would have.
3. **Next week.** Birthdays, guests, a school trip that needs a packed lunch. Plan them into the shopping list now.

At the end of the month, compare the plan with what happened and adjust next month's number. In Bill Tracker, the insights show your top spending categories and the change since the previous month, and you can filter by envelope or person to see who did which shops.

## Ways to keep groceries inside the budget

None of these are rules — pick the two or three that fit your family:

- **Plan the week's dinners before the big shop**, and write the list from the plan.
- **Shop once, top up once.** Fewer trips means fewer extras.
- **Cook double and freeze half** on a calmer day.
- **Check what's already in the fridge** before writing the list.
- **Give the kids a small snack allowance** inside the grocery envelope, so the "can I have" conversation has an answer.
- **Keep non-food buys visible.** If toiletries and cleaning are bought with the food, decide whether they share the envelope or get their own.

## FAQ

### How much should a family of 4 spend on groceries a month?

There's no single right amount — prices, children's ages and how much you cook all change it. Add up two months of your own grocery spending, take the average and trim it slightly. That number fits your family far better than a national average.

### How do I make a weekly grocery budget?

Take your monthly grocery budget and divide it across the weeks from payday to payday, giving the first week a little more for the stock-up shop. Write the weekly amounts down and compare them with what's left in your grocery envelope each week.

### Should toiletries and cleaning products be in the grocery budget?

Either works, as long as you decide once. If you buy them in the same shop, keeping them in the grocery envelope is simpler. If you want to see food costs on their own, give them a separate envelope.

### What do we do when the grocery money runs out before payday?

Move money from another envelope and agree which one — eating out is the usual candidate. If it happens every month, the budget is too low: raise it to match reality and cut somewhere else instead.
