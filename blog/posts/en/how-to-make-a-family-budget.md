To make a family budget, write down what your household really brings home in a month, split it into 8 to 10 categories ("envelopes") with a limit each, and make sure the limits add up to exactly that income. Then log every purchase as it happens and look at the numbers together once a week. The first month will be rough; by the third, the plan will fit your family.

What makes a family budget different from a personal one is not the maths. It's that several people spend from the same money, kids bring costs nobody planned, and the big bills arrive once a year. The steps below are built around those three problems.

## Step 1: Start from the money that actually arrives

Take the amount that lands in your accounts in a normal month, after tax and deductions. Add child benefit or other regular payments if you receive them. Leave out bonuses, tax refunds and anything you are not sure about — when they come, they are a bonus, not a hole you have to fill.

If one income changes month to month (shifts, commission, freelance work), use the lowest of the last three months. Budgeting on a good month is the fastest way to be short on the 20th.

Then pick the day your month starts. If the main salary arrives on the 25th, run your budget month from the 25th to the 24th. Rent, school fees and other bills will then fall inside the month that pays for them.

## Step 2: Choose envelopes that match family life

An envelope is a category with a limit. The paper version is literal — cash in labelled envelopes — but the idea works the same on a phone or in a notebook. For a family, a good starting set is:

1. **Housing and bills** — rent or mortgage, energy, water, internet, phones
2. **Groceries** — food, toiletries, household supplies
3. **Transport** — fuel, public transport, parking
4. **Kids** — school costs, clubs, clothes, pocket money
5. **Health** — pharmacy, glasses, dentist
6. **Eating out and treats** — takeaway, ice cream, the cinema
7. **Yearly costs** — see step 4
8. **Savings**
9. **Personal money** — a fixed amount for each adult, no questions asked
10. **Everything else**

Ten is plenty. With more, you start hesitating at every purchase about where it belongs, and a budget that needs thinking at the till doesn't survive.

## Step 3: Put numbers on them — from last month, not from hope

Open the last one or two months of bank and card statements and sort the spending into your envelopes. It takes an hour, and it is the only part that feels like work. Then trim gently: 10–15% off the envelopes that ran high, not 50%.

A worked example for a family of four with a combined take-home pay of 5,600 a month:

| Envelope | Monthly budget |
|---|---|
| Housing and bills | 1,960 |
| Groceries | 950 |
| Transport | 420 |
| Kids | 380 |
| Health | 120 |
| Eating out and treats | 250 |
| Yearly costs | 400 |
| Savings | 500 |
| Personal money (2 × 100) | 200 |
| Everything else | 420 |
| **Total** | **5,600** |

The total matches the income exactly. Every unit of money has a job, including the part you save. If your envelopes add up to more than you earn, cut now, on paper — not in the third week, at the supermarket.

## Step 4: Give yearly costs their own envelope

Most family budgets don't break on groceries. They break in the month with the car insurance, the summer holiday or the school shoe shopping. These costs are not surprises; they just don't come every month.

List everything you pay once or twice a year and divide the total by twelve:

| Yearly cost | Per year |
|---|---|
| Car insurance | 720 |
| Summer holiday | 2,400 |
| Birthdays, holidays and gifts | 900 |
| Car service and repairs | 480 |
| Back to school | 300 |
| **Total** | **4,800** |

4,800 ÷ 12 = 400 a month — the "Yearly costs" envelope in the table above. Each month, move that 400 to a separate savings account or simply leave it unspent and note the running total. When the insurance bill arrives, the money is already there.

## Step 5: Make logging a family habit

A budget is only as good as its records. What works for families:

- **Log at the till.** Ten seconds now beats twenty minutes of guessing on Sunday.
- **Every adult logs.** If one parent keeps the budget for everyone, it is always out of date, and the other parent can't make decisions in a shop.
- **Set fixed bills to repeat.** Rent, salary, internet and subscriptions are the same every month; enter them once.
- **Let older kids see their envelope.** A teenager with a visible clothes budget learns more from it than from any lecture.

## Step 6: A 15-minute family check every week

Pick a fixed moment, such as Sunday breakfast, and look at three things:

1. **Pace.** If it's the 12th and groceries is 70% gone, plan the next two weeks now — more home cooking, fewer top-up shops.
2. **Moves.** Shifting money from one envelope to another is not failure; it's the method working. Just agree on it together.
3. **What's coming.** A school trip, a birthday party, a car inspection. If it has no envelope, decide now where the money comes from.

At the end of the month, compare plan and reality and adjust next month's amounts. The method only works if the numbers keep moving towards the truth.

## Common mistakes in a family budget

- **No personal money.** A budget where every coffee needs justifying feels like surveillance, and people quietly stop logging.
- **Forgetting the kids' "small" costs.** School photos, class gifts and club fees are small one by one and large together. Give the kids' envelope a realistic number.
- **Treating one bad month as proof it doesn't work.** A blown envelope is information. Move money, adjust next month, carry on.
- **Too many envelopes too soon.** Start with ten. Split one only when it keeps hiding something you want to see.

## FAQ

### How much should a family spend on groceries?

There is no correct number — it depends on where you live, how many children you have and how much you cook. Use your own last two months as the starting point, then adjust. A grocery envelope that lasts the whole month is the right size for your family.

### What's the best budgeting method for a family?

The one everyone actually follows. Envelopes work well for families because the limits are easy to explain, even to children: "there's 60 left for eating out this month". Percentage rules like 50/30/20 can be a useful check, but envelopes tell you what to do today.

### Should children be involved in the family budget?

Older children benefit from seeing part of it, especially an envelope that is about them — clothes, activities or pocket money. They don't need to see salaries. Seeing that a limit exists and how it's used is the lesson.

### What do we do when an unexpected expense comes up?

Take the money from another envelope and agree which one. If it happens often, it isn't unexpected — give it its own envelope or add it to yearly costs.
