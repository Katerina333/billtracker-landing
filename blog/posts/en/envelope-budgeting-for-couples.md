Envelope budgeting for couples works like this: you split the month's money into a handful of named envelopes — groceries, rent, eating out — agree on an amount for each, and both write down what you spend. When an envelope is empty, you stop or you move money from another one, together. It takes one evening to set up and about ten seconds per purchase to keep going.

The method is older than banking apps. People used to put cash into paper envelopes on payday. The paper is optional now, but the idea still does the one thing most budgets fail at: it tells you *before* you spend whether you can afford it.

## Why envelopes suit two people

A budget shared by two people breaks for boring reasons. One person tracks, the other doesn't. Nobody knows what "we're doing fine this month" means. A purchase one of you considers normal feels like a surprise to the other.

Envelopes fix this because the agreement happens once, at the start of the month, and not at the checkout:

- **The limit is visible.** "There's 80 left for eating out" is a fact, not an opinion.
- **Small spending needs no discussion.** If it fits in the envelope, it's already agreed.
- **Arguments become trade-offs.** Not "you spend too much" but "do we take it from holidays or from clothes?"

## Step 1: Add up what actually comes in

Use the money that lands in your accounts in a normal month, after tax. If one income varies, take the lowest of the last three months. Budget on that number and treat anything above it as extra.

Decide when your month starts. If you're both paid on the 25th, a budget month running from the 25th to the 24th is much easier to follow than a calendar month.

## Step 2: Choose 6 to 10 envelopes

Fewer is better. With thirty envelopes you'll spend the month deciding where a purchase belongs. A good first set for most households:

1. Housing (rent or mortgage, utilities)
2. Groceries
3. Transport
4. Eating out and takeaway
5. Kids or pets, if you have them
6. Health
7. Subscriptions and phone
8. Fun money — one envelope each
9. Savings

The two personal envelopes matter more than they look. Each of you gets an amount that the other person doesn't comment on. It keeps a shared budget from feeling like surveillance.

## Step 3: Put numbers on them — from last month, not from hope

Open last month's bank and card statements and sort the spending into your envelopes. This takes about half an hour and is the only tedious part.

A worked example. Two people bring home 4,200 a month between them:

| Envelope | Last month | This month's budget |
|---|---|---|
| Housing and utilities | 1,450 | 1,450 |
| Groceries | 690 | 650 |
| Transport | 310 | 300 |
| Eating out | 420 | 300 |
| Health | 90 | 100 |
| Subscriptions and phone | 135 | 120 |
| Fun money (2 × 150) | — | 300 |
| Savings | 200 | 500 |
| Everything else | 640 | 480 |
| **Total** | | **4,200** |

Two things to notice. The total equals the income exactly: every unit of money has a job. And the cuts are modest. Dropping eating out from 420 to 300 is realistic; dropping it to 100 means the budget is abandoned by the 12th.

Keep an "everything else" envelope in the first months. When you see what lands there, you'll know which new envelope you need.

## Step 4: Log every expense when it happens

This is where budgets live or die. Rules that work for couples:

- **Log at the till, not on Sunday.** Nobody remembers Tuesday's bakery on Sunday.
- **Both of you log.** A budget one person keeps for two is always out of date.
- **Fixed bills shouldn't need logging.** Rent, salary and subscriptions are the same every month — set them up once to repeat.

## Step 5: A ten-minute check once a week

Pick a fixed moment — Sunday coffee works. Look at three things:

1. Which envelopes are ahead of the calendar? If it's the 10th and groceries is 60% spent, plan for it now.
2. Does anything need moving? Moving money between envelopes is not cheating. It's the method working. Just do it together.
3. Is anything coming that has no envelope — a birthday, a car inspection?

At the end of the month, compare budget and reality, and adjust next month's amounts. Expect the first month to be wrong. By the third, the numbers settle.

## Common mistakes

- **Budgeting the month you wish you had.** Start from real spending and trim by 10–15%, not by half.
- **Forgetting yearly costs.** Insurance, holidays and gifts arrive every year and still surprise people. Divide them by twelve and give them an envelope.
- **One person as the accountant.** If only one of you sees the numbers, the other can't make good decisions in a shop.
- **Giving up after one blown envelope.** Overspending on one line is information. Move money and carry on.

## FAQ

### Do we need a joint bank account?

No. Envelopes describe what the money is for, not where it sits. Many couples keep separate accounts and one shared budget. What matters is that both of you see the same envelope totals.

### What if we earn different amounts?

The budget doesn't care who earned what. Decide separately how each of you contributes — equal amounts or in proportion to income — and then budget the combined total. Equal fun-money envelopes are a common choice even when incomes differ.

### Should we use cash or an app?

Cash makes the limit physical, which helps with envelopes that keep running over, such as eating out. An app is easier for card payments and for two people in different places. Some couples mix: cash for one or two problem envelopes, an app for the rest.

### How many envelopes is too many?

When you hesitate about where a purchase goes, you have too many. Most households manage well with 8 to 12. Bill Tracker's free plan covers 10 envelopes and two people in one space, which is enough for a first shared budget.

### What do we do with money left at the end of the month?

Decide the rule in advance: move it to savings, keep it in the same envelope for next month, or split it. Leftovers in a holiday or gift envelope are usually worth keeping where they are — that's what those envelopes are for.
