Good envelope budgeting categories follow your real spending, not a template: most households need 6 to 10 envelopes covering housing, groceries, bills, transport, irregular yearly costs, savings, personal money and a small buffer. Start with those, give each one a monthly limit that adds up to your take-home pay, and split an envelope only when it keeps hiding something you want to see.

Below: the starter list, the three kinds of envelopes, the categories people forget, a full month in numbers and how to set it all up in a few minutes.

## Envelope budgeting categories: the starter list

These ten envelopes cover almost every household. Rename them in your own words — "Food shop" or "Supermarket" works as well as "Groceries", as long as everyone who spends knows what goes where.

1. **Housing** — rent or mortgage payment, building fees
2. **Groceries** — food and household supplies from any shop
3. **Bills** — electricity, heating, water, internet, phones, streaming
4. **Transport** — fuel, tickets, parking, taxis
5. **Kids** — childcare, school costs, clubs, clothes
6. **Yearly costs** — insurance, car service, gifts, holidays: anything that doesn't come every month
7. **Savings** — the amount you put aside on payday, before spending starts
8. **Personal money** — a fixed amount per adult, spent without explanation
9. **Eating out and fun** — restaurants, takeaway, cinema, hobbies
10. **Buffer** — small surprises: a broken charger, a forgotten birthday, a parking fine

No kids? Drop that envelope. A pet, a car loan or a long commute? Give it its own envelope. The list is a starting point, not a rule.

## Three kinds of envelopes: fixed, variable and irregular

Sorting your categories into three groups makes the limits much easier to set.

**Fixed envelopes** (housing, bills, loan payments) cost roughly the same every month. Set the limit to the actual amount and stop thinking about it. These are good candidates for recurring entries, so they are logged without anyone typing them.

**Variable envelopes** (groceries, transport, eating out, personal money) are where budgets are won or lost. Set the limit from last month's real spending, then adjust once you have two or three months of data. These are the envelopes worth checking mid-month.

**Irregular envelopes** (yearly costs, buffer) collect money every month for bills that arrive once or twice a year. Add up next year's known irregular costs, divide by twelve, and feed that amount into the envelope. When the car insurance arrives, the money is already there.

## How many envelopes should you have?

Six to ten. Fewer than six and one envelope — usually "Everything else" — swallows half the budget and tells you nothing. More than twelve and you hesitate at the till about which envelope a purchase belongs to, and logging slows down.

A simple test: if you can't decide in two seconds where a purchase goes, two of your envelopes overlap. Merge them. If one envelope runs out every month and you can't see why, split it — "Groceries" into "Supermarket" and "Top-up shops", for example — for a month or two until you find the leak.

## The six-envelope system

Some people start with just six broad envelopes: necessities, long-term savings, education, fun, giving and a "freedom" pot for the future. It's a quick way to try the method in one evening. The catch is that "necessities" ends up holding rent, food, bills and transport together, so you can't see which one is growing. Once you have a month of data, splitting necessities into three or four envelopes usually gives clearer answers.

## Envelopes people forget

The categories that break a first budget are rarely the big ones. Watch for:

- **Gifts and celebrations** — birthdays, weddings, the end-of-year holidays
- **Annual subscriptions and fees** — insurance, licences, memberships, software
- **Car and home maintenance** — tyres, a service, a dripping tap
- **Health** — dentist, glasses, pharmacy
- **Back-to-school** — books, shoes, a new school bag every autumn

You don't need an envelope for each. Most fit into **Yearly costs**; the rest into **Buffer**.

## A worked example: ten envelopes for one month

A family of three with a take-home pay of 2,800 a month, budget month starting on payday:

| Envelope | Monthly limit |
|---|---|
| Housing | 950 |
| Groceries | 480 |
| Bills | 220 |
| Transport | 160 |
| Kids | 150 |
| Yearly costs | 200 |
| Savings | 300 |
| Personal money (2 × 70) | 140 |
| Eating out and fun | 120 |
| Buffer | 80 |
| **Total** | **2,800** |

Every unit of income has a job, so nothing is left "floating". Three of the envelopes (housing, bills, savings) need little attention once they're set; bills only change with the seasons. Two (yearly costs, buffer) build up as the family sets money aside each month. The other five are the ones this family checks once a week. If 300 of Groceries is gone 12 days into the budget month, they know with almost three weeks to spare, and can move 30 from eating out instead of finding out on the last day.

## FAQ

### What are the most common envelope budgeting categories?

Housing, groceries, bills, transport, savings, personal money and eating out are in almost every envelope budget. Add kids, pets or a car if they apply to you, plus a yearly-costs envelope and a small buffer for surprises.

### Should savings be an envelope?

Yes. Treating savings as an envelope that is filled on payday, rather than whatever is left at the end of the month, is what makes it happen. Move the money to a savings account straight away if that helps you leave it alone.

### What goes in a miscellaneous envelope?

As little as possible. Call it "Buffer", keep it small, and look at what lands there each month. If one kind of spending keeps appearing — gifts, say — give it its own envelope or move it to yearly costs.

### Can two people share the same envelope categories?

Yes, and it works better than two separate budgets for shared costs. Agree on the shared envelopes together, give each adult a personal-money envelope, and log into one shared budget so both of you see the same numbers.
