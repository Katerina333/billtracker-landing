To track monthly expenses, choose one place to write them down, sort every purchase into one of 8 to 10 categories, and log it within a minute of paying — card or cash. Once a week, spend ten minutes looking at the totals; at the end of the month, compare them with what came in. You don't need to connect your bank for any of this, and typing each expense yourself is exactly what makes you notice where the money goes.

Most people who give up on tracking don't fail at the maths. They fail at the habit: too many categories, a system that only works at a desk, or a backlog of receipts nobody wants to sort on Sunday. The steps below are built to avoid those three traps.

## Why track expenses by hand instead of linking your bank

Automatic imports sound easier, and for some people they are. But a list that fills itself is easy to ignore. When you type "4.50, coffee" yourself, you feel the purchase a second time — and that small moment is where spending habits actually change.

Tracking by hand also covers things a bank feed can't:

- **Cash.** Market stalls, pocket money, the window cleaner. A bank only sees the withdrawal, not where the cash went.
- **Shared spending.** If two people pay for the household from different accounts, no single bank feed shows the whole picture.
- **Privacy.** Nobody gets your bank login, and nothing has to be shared with a third party.

The trade-off is discipline: a manual tracker is only as good as the habit behind it. That's what the rest of this guide is about.

## Step 1: Pick one place — and only one

A notebook, a spreadsheet or a phone app all work. What doesn't work is a mix: half the purchases in a notes app, a few on receipts in a drawer, the rest "in your head". Choose the tool you will have with you when you pay. For most people that means the phone, because it's the only thing always in their pocket.

If you share costs with a partner or family, choose a place everyone can write to. A tracker kept by one person for a whole household is always a few days behind.

## Step 2: Use 8 to 10 categories

Categories make the numbers useful: "groceries 610" tells you something, a list of 58 separate purchases doesn't. A good starting set:

1. **Housing and bills** — rent or mortgage, energy, water, internet
2. **Groceries** — food and household supplies
3. **Transport** — fuel, tickets, parking
4. **Eating out and coffee**
5. **Health** — pharmacy, dentist, glasses
6. **Subscriptions and phone**
7. **Clothes**
8. **Gifts and other**

Resist the urge to add more in week one. If "other" keeps growing, look at what's inside it at the end of the month and split off a new category then.

## Step 3: Log every expense within a minute

This is the whole method. Amount, category, done — a note only if you'll need it later. A few rules that keep it going:

- **Log at the till, not at the end of the day.** By evening you'll have forgotten the parking and the bakery.
- **Cash counts the same as card.** When you withdraw cash, don't log the withdrawal as spending — log what you buy with it.
- **One purchase, one category.** If the supermarket receipt has a phone charger on it, don't split it. Use the main category and move on; perfect is the enemy of done.
- **Missed one? Estimate it.** A rough "about 12, lunch" is better than a gap.

## Step 4: Enter fixed bills once

Rent, energy, internet, insurance and subscriptions are the same (or close to it) every month. Enter them on the day they leave your account, or set them to repeat if your tool allows it. Then your daily logging is only the variable spending — the part you can actually change.

## Step 5: A 10-minute check every week

Pick a fixed moment, such as Sunday morning, and look at three things:

1. **Totals per category so far.** Is anything already higher than last month at the same point?
2. **Gaps.** Compare your tracked total with your account balance. If the balance fell by more than you logged, something is missing — usually cash or a card payment you forgot.
3. **The week ahead.** Birthdays, a car service, a school trip. Knowing they're coming is half the job.

## A worked example: one month of tracked expenses

Here is what a month looks like for one person with a take-home pay of 3,200, after four weeks of logging:

| Category | Spent | Entries |
|---|---|---|
| Housing and bills | 1,250 | 4 |
| Groceries | 610 | 18 |
| Transport | 230 | 9 |
| Eating out and coffee | 185 | 11 |
| Health | 45 | 3 |
| Subscriptions and phone | 62 | 5 |
| Clothes | 90 | 2 |
| Gifts and other | 128 | 6 |
| **Total** | **2,600** | **58** |

Three things jump out. Groceries took 18 trips — fewer, bigger shops would probably cost less. Eating out and coffee was 11 small entries that nobody would have guessed added up to 185. And 600 of the 3,200 is left: now there's a real number to decide about, instead of a vague feeling that "money disappears".

After one or two months of tracking, you know what your life actually costs. ## Common mistakes when tracking expenses

- **Starting with 25 categories.** Every purchase becomes a decision, and the habit dies in the second week.
- **Saving receipts "to enter later".** Later rarely comes. Log now, throw the receipt away.
- **Only tracking card payments.** Cash spending is often where the surprises are.
- **Judging every entry.** Tracking is about seeing, not about guilt. A week with too many takeaways is information, not failure.
- **Stopping after a bad week.** Missed three days? Estimate what you can, and pick it up again today.

## FAQ

### Do I need to connect my bank to track my expenses?

No. Writing each expense down yourself — in a notebook, a spreadsheet or an app without bank access — gives you the same totals, includes cash, and keeps your bank login private. It takes a few seconds per purchase.

### How long should I track expenses before making a budget?

One full month gives you a usable picture; two months smooth out one-off costs. You can start setting limits after the first month and adjust them as you learn more.

### What's the easiest way to track cash spending?

Log the purchase, not the withdrawal. When you buy something with cash, enter the amount and category straight away, just as you would with a card. If you forget, compare the cash left in your wallet with what you logged at the weekly check.

### Should I track every small purchase?

Yes, at least at the start. Small, frequent purchases — coffee, snacks, parking — are exactly the ones people underestimate. Once you know your patterns, you can decide which ones matter.
